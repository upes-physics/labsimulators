Aim of the experiment

Determine the optical energy band gap of a direct-band-gap semiconductor from its UV–Visible absorption spectrum, and compare the optical edge for the four measured particle-size ranges.

What you will measure

The supplied experimental observations contain wavelength, absorbance, photon energy, and the direct-transition Tauc ordinate. The simulator plots these measured values and performs a least-squares fit over the selected energy interval.

Learning outcomes

Absorbance and the supplied Tauc data

Absorbance A measures the attenuation of incident light at each wavelength. The experimental workbook also supplies photon energy and the direct-transition Tauc ordinate, so the simulator does not introduce an adjustable thickness or an arbitrary absorbance multiplier.

A = −log₁₀(I/I₀),   T = I/I₀ = 10−A
hν (eV) = 1240/λ (nm)

Data rule: the measured absorbance and (αhν)² values are plotted without synthetic scaling or random noise.

Photon energy and band gap

Photon energy increases as wavelength decreases. Absorption rises strongly when photons have enough energy to promote electrons across the forbidden gap.

hν (eV) = 1240/λ (nm)
Direct: (αhν)² = C(hν − Eg)
Indirect: (αhν)½ = C(hν − Eg)

Electronic bands and the absorption edge

In a solid, closely spaced atomic energy levels form allowed bands. The valence band is normally occupied, while the conduction band contains mobile states that support electrical conduction. The interval between them contains no allowed states and is called the band gap.

When hν < Eg, an ideal semiconductor cannot absorb the photon through a band-to-band transition. When hν ≥ Eg, an electron can be excited into the conduction band, leaving a hole in the valence band. The resulting rapid increase of α is the optical absorption edge.

Real spectra may show a gradual tail below the main edge because of disorder, defects, impurities, scattering, or instrumental background.

Why the Tauc transformation works

Near the absorption edge, the absorption coefficient can be written in the general form

(αhν)r = C(hν − Eg)

The exponent is chosen for the transition mechanism. This simulator uses r = 2 for an allowed direct transition and r = ½ for an allowed indirect transition. A direct transition conserves crystal momentum without a phonon; an indirect transition also requires phonon participation.

After the correct transformation, the edge region becomes approximately linear. Extending this line to (αhν)r = 0 gives the optical gap.

Particle size and quantum confinement (Brus equation)

For particles much larger than the relevant carrier length scale, the band gap approaches its bulk value and is essentially independent of geometrical size. When the particle radius becomes comparable with the exciton Bohr radius, electrons and holes are spatially confined. Their allowed energies, given by the Brus equation, become more widely separated.

Eg(R) ≈ Eg,bulk + (ℏ²π²/2R²)(1/me* + 1/mh*) − Coulomb term

The dominant confinement term varies approximately as 1/R², so smaller nanocrystals generally show a larger optical gap and a blue-shifted absorption edge. Peak widths and intensities may also change because the density of states, surface contribution, size distribution, and oscillator strength are different.

Interpreting the four experimental datasets

Particle-size range: each selection loads an independent measured spectrum. The edge position, absorbance values, and Tauc values therefore change together rather than through artificial vertical scaling.

Quantum-confinement trend: the smaller-particle datasets show the absorption edge at higher photon energy. This produces a larger fitted optical gap than in the larger-particle datasets.

Fit interval: the initial interval is chosen from the approximately linear rising part of each supplied Tauc dataset. Students may change its limits to see how point selection affects the extrapolated intercept and R².

Measurement limitations: the fitted gap still depends on spectral sampling, sample preparation, scattering, baseline correction, and the physical validity of the selected transition model.

Principle and equations

A UV–Visible spectrometer compares transmitted intensity I with incident intensity I₀. The optical band gap is obtained by extrapolating the linear Tauc region to the energy axis.

A = −log₁₀(I/I₀),   T = 10−A
hν (eV) = 1240/λ (nm)
(αhν)² = C(hν − Eg)

Interpretation. The fitted line is y = mhν + c; therefore its energy-axis intercept is Eg = −c/m. The recommended-fit value is calculated from the default linear interval for each measured dataset.
