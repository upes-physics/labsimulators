Hall Effect Virtual Laboratory

Determine the Hall coefficient, carrier type, and carrier density of a semiconductor from transverse-voltage measurements.

Lorentz force · Hall voltage · carrier sign · two independent slope methods

Aim and apparatus

Aim: Study the Hall effect and determine the Hall coefficient RH and carrier density n of a semiconductor.

Apparatus: Ge Hall probe of thickness 0.4–0.5 mm, InAs magnetic-field probe, Hall-effect setup, digital millivoltmeter, constant-current source, and electromagnet capable of approximately 11,000 G.

Learning outcomes

  • Explain transverse charge separation using the Lorentz force.
  • Identify electrons or holes from the Hall-voltage sign.
  • Correct observations for zero-field offset.
  • Determine RH using both required graphs.
  • Calculate carrier density and judge linearity.

Experimental procedure

  1. Connect the lengthwise contacts to the constant-current terminals and the widthwise contacts to the millivoltmeter.
  2. With the probe outside the magnetic field, record the zero-field potential. Subtract it from subsequent voltage readings.
  3. Place the Hall probe perpendicular to the magnetic field and measure the field with the InAs probe.
  4. At two constant magnetic fields, vary current and record VH.
  5. At two constant currents, vary magnetic field and record VH.
  6. Fit both sets of graphs, calculate RH by the two methods, take the mean, and calculate carrier density.

Precautions

  • Keep the Hall probe perpendicular to the magnetic field and centred between the pole pieces.
  • Do not exceed the current rating of the semiconductor probe.
  • Measure the small Hall voltage carefully and correct the zero-field potential.
  • Change electromagnet current gradually to protect the coils and reduce hysteresis-related errors.
  • Keep temperature reasonably constant because carrier density and mobility are temperature dependent.

Microscopic origin

Charge carriers moving with drift velocity v in a magnetic field B experience the Lorentz force. When current is along x and field is along z, the magnetic force acts along y and pushes carriers toward one side of the specimen.

F = q(E + v × B)

Accumulated charge creates a transverse electric field. Equilibrium is reached when the electric force balances the magnetic force, producing a measurable Hall voltage across the width.

Hall coefficient and carrier density

For a rectangular specimen carrying current I in perpendicular field B (denoted Hz in the laboratory manual), the transverse voltage is

VH = RHIB/t
RH = VHt/(IB)

In the single-carrier approximation, RH = 1/(nq). Its sign identifies the dominant carrier: negative for electrons and positive for holes. Carrier density uses the magnitude:

n = 1/(|RH|e),   e = 1.602 × 10−19 C

Practical-unit conversion

The laboratory records VH in mV, thickness t in cm, current in mA, and magnetic field in gauss. Since 1 G = 10−4 T, the directly usable expression is

RH(cm³/C) = 108 VH(mV)t(cm) / [I(mA)B(G)]

Always use the corrected Hall voltage. Mixing SI and laboratory units without the factor 108 produces an error of many orders of magnitude.

Sign, offset, and reversal tests

The measured voltage can contain contact misalignment, thermoelectric voltage, and meter offset even at B = 0. Subtracting the zero-field potential removes the simplest constant offset.

A strong experimental check is to reverse B or I. The true Hall voltage changes sign, whereas many offsets do not. Half the difference between positive and negative readings isolates the odd Hall component.

VH = [V(+B) − V(−B)]/2

Assumptions and limitations

The relation RH = 1/(nq) assumes one dominant carrier type with uniform density. In compensated or multiband semiconductors, electrons and holes can both contribute, so the measured Hall coefficient becomes mobility weighted and the simple density is an effective value.

The ideal model also assumes uniform thickness, homogeneous current density, accurate field calibration, negligible magnetoresistance mixing, ohmic contacts, and constant temperature. Probe rotation reduces the perpendicular field by cosφ; thickness error transfers directly to RH.

The Hall effect is widely used for carrier characterization, magnetic-field sensing, current sensing, position and speed detection, brushless-motor commutation, and automotive and aerospace sensors.

Carrier deflection and Hall-field formation

Watch carriers drift along the sample and deflect under q(v × B). Charge accumulation creates the transverse Hall field. Changing carrier type or magnetic-field direction reverses the polarity.
